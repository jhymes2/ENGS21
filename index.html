On Belay?

An automated climbing wall sanitization system

ENGS 21 — Dartmouth Engineering
Problem State of Art Solution Specs Prototypes Testing Ethics Business Economics Takeaways Hardware Team

The Problem

Indoor climbing gyms are difficult to clean, resulting in infrequent cleaning and the buildup of bacteria that can lead to infections. In 2021, over 5.6 million people visited climbing gyms. With 695 commercial climbing gyms in the US, there are many people who can benefit from our product. Two climbers on our team have personally suffered from climbing-wall-born infections from hobby climbing.

Climbing gym walls have been described as a "fecal veneer" by researchers (Bräuer et al., 2014). Gyms are incentivized to keep a sanitary facility, as healthy climbers are ones that will keep coming back. Private and international climbing gyms offer an additional market, though one that could be more difficult to break into.

Our users are climbing gym employees. The product will be purchased by climbing gym owners. The primary beneficiary will be clients, though this benefit to clients will also monetarily benefit gym owners due to the marketability of a sanitary facility.

"Both existing solutions result in infrequent cleanings or a lack of thorough sanitation."

State of the Art

The current state of the art for climbing wall cleaning is the removal of all climbing holds, followed by the removal of all wall panels, transportation of those panels to an alternate location, power washing, and then reinstallment. This process can easily take all day or longer and is usually only done once a year since it requires closing the gym and a lot of manual effort.

Another option is to belay an individual up the wall with a soap and/or vinegar solution with a rag to clean manually. This process is also highly time-intensive — some climbing gym panels can be upwards of 100 sq ft — and scrubbing an entire gym is a major undertaking.

Automated wall cleaning technology has been explored in other areas: window cleaning robots and pool cleaning robots have been patented. These devices, however, do not work for climbing gyms — Patent Application No. 18/562,897 utilizes a massive liquid spraying system which would create a mess indoors, and Patent No. US 9689170 B1 uses a large fan that would put chalk and debris into the air. While these existing devices served as inspirations, none can be directly used in indoor climbing gym wall sanitation.

Our Solution

On Belay? is a rope-mounted cleaning device that clips into the existing top-rope belay infrastructure already present in every climbing gym. The device is raised to the top of the wall, then belayed down by an operator as the roller brush cleans all dust and the spray module disinfects. No new rail infrastructure is required.

The final prototype consists of four subsystems: mechanical (roller & belay), electrical, and fluid. A relay system controls the brush and sprayer, activating them once the device is in position — preventing mess during setup. The device is controlled via an Arduino Nano managing relay outputs for the belay mechanism, brush motor, spray solenoid, and status light.

Ease of use and sanitation were the main driving factors. Damage-free was also heavily weighted since it would be easier to convince climbing gyms to test and purchase a product with no risk to their facility.

Design Specifications

Ease of Use

< 15 min setup, single operator

Addresses the core labor-intensity of current methods. One person sets up and cleans a panel in under 15 minutes.

Efficacy

> 80% bacteria reduction

Kills pathogens and removes visible chalk buildup. Tested via swab and culture comparison.

Damage-Free

No visible wall damage

Critical for gym adoption. Walls are visually inspected after each use to confirm no damage.

Speed

< 15 min per 30 sq ft section

Targets the time-intensive nature of current cleaning. Measured via stopwatch on multiple runs.

Durability

Survive 50+ cleaning sessions

Stress tested for reliable operation. Drop test from 15 ft used as durability benchmark.

Cost

< $300 total system

Economically viable for commercial gym purchase. Mass production cost targeted at $200/unit.

Safety

No slips or drops during testing

Disinfectant handling, chalk dust ventilation, electronics. Pulley system generates enough friction to hold device.

Usability

Operate on 40° wall angles

Tested by belaying the device down various sections at Dartmouth and The Nook Climbing gyms.

Full System with Spray Module

Parts Iteration

Rope system: During testing it became clear that a 1-rope system, though easier to use, could not properly tension the device to the wall. This led to the two-rope system present in the final prototype.

📷 1-Rope System (left) vs. 2-Rope System (right)

Brush arms: Initially beginning with wooden laser-cut arms, the team transitioned to machined metal arms hoping to improve durability. During testing, these arms failed to properly angle the brush to be in contact with the wall. Through trial and error, the team worked through many different arms until finding the correct shape and size.

📷 Brush Arm Iterations (various)
📷 Incorrect Brush Arm Angle

Spraying mechanisms: Two different 3D-printed spray attachments were designed, both of which were not fully watertight. Out of time, the team mounted the attachments and taped existing spray nozzles to the side. Full integration of these attachments is planned for future revisions.

Testing & Results

The team refined specifications as prototyping progressed, tailoring benchmarks to the actual device. Ease of use became a measure of headcount required; speed became a specific sq ft/min metric; setup time was added; safety was tailored to measure slips during device operation; durability became a drop test; usability measured wall angle capability.

Sustainability & Ethics

The eco-design strategy wheel shows strong performance across most dimensions: all primary materials (wood, aluminum, metal) are recyclable; manufacturing uses CNC and laser-cutting processes with minimal parts; the device runs on a rechargeable 12V battery with minimal disinfectant waste; and it is designed for longevity with replaceable parts and reuse at end-of-life. Parts can be replaced, the battery recharged, and the reservoir refilled.

The main sustainability weaknesses are the battery and plastic injection-molded components, which could be improved through certified-recyclable sourcing or material substitution. All materials used (wood, metal, plastic) are common and not subject to supply concerns.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical concerns center primarily around safety: the device could fall from height onto a person, or expose users to cleaning chemicals. Both risks are mitigated by restricting operation to belay-certified staff. A pulley system generates enough friction to hold the device securely, and 3D-printed covers protect exposed chains, brush, and electronics.

The device has no foreseeable unintended uses outside of climbing gyms — the belay infrastructure required to operate it exists only in climbing facilities. The product will be available to all commercial and private gyms at a commercially reasonable price point. No ethical concerns beyond standard safety protocols were identified.

Improvements & Takeaways

The team identified several areas for improvement and drew important lessons from the challenges encountered during design, fabrication, and testing.

Areas for Improvement

  • Enable the device to handle more overhung wall angles (currently limited to ~5°)
  • Increase durability by replacing wood and 3D-printed parts with metal and robust plastics
  • Add automatic on/off functionality (currently requires programming per device sold)
  • Widen the cleaning surface to cover a greater wall area per pass
  • Reduce device weight (users flagged it as too heavy)
  • Simplify the two-rope system (users found it unintuitive)
  • Add automatic lowering/raising for hands-free operation
  • Fully integrate the spray nozzle attachments (currently taped)

Electronics & Hardware

The control system runs on an Arduino Nano (ATmega328) via PlatformIO. Four digital outputs manage the core actuators through relay modules:

ConstantPinFunction
PIN_RELAY_BELAYD2Belay motor relay
PIN_RELAY_BRUSHD3Roller brush motor relay
PIN_RELAY_SPRAYD4Disinfectant spray solenoid relay
PIN_SIGNAL_LIGHTD13 (LED_BUILTIN)Status indicator light

The relay system is key to preventing mess: the brush and spray module are only activated once the device is in position on the wall, not during the raising phase. Built with the Arduino framework. Source available on GitHub.
