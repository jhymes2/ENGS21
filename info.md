Problem Statement:
Indoor climbing gym walls are difficult to clean, resulting in infrequent cleaning and allowing the buildup of bacteria that can lead to infections. 
Climbing walls are typically cleaned in a few different ways, but both are lengthy and difficult for employees. One study even found a “fecal veneer” present on each climbing wall that they tested (Bräuer et al., 2014). Bacteria can build up on climbing walls when climbers wear their climbing shoes into the restroom, where bacteria are more present, and then spread when they touch the walls with unwashed hands or climbing shoes. Additional bacteria can come from climbers coughing or sneezing while climbing on the wall. Furthermore, climbers use chalk to increase their grip on holds, and chalk residue can be left on the holds and the climbing wall itself. Proper cleaning of climbing walls involves both removing the chalk residue and disinfecting the wall.
The problem affects more than the general cleanliness of the gym. Indoor climbing gyms are used recreationally and competitively. There are over 695 commercial climbing gyms operating in the US in 2026 (Climbing Business Journal, 2026) and over 5.6 million climbers took to indoor gyms in 2021 (Climbing Business Journal, 2022), making for a vast user-base, and large potential to be exposed to harmful bacteria. With members of our team having developed infections from climbing walls, this problem has personal significance to us.
While climbers are the group most affected by bacteria as a result of infrequent cleanings, the users for any solution that seeks to address this problem are the employees of the climbing gym. Current cleaning methods are time intensive and difficult, which results in the task being put off or scheduled infrequently. Commercial climbing gyms will be the purchaser for the solution to this problem, with the intention of improving their sanitation, giving their climbers a better experience, and streamlining the tasks of their employees.
State of the Art
Existing solutions for climbing wall sanitation are minimal. Some gyms, when they remove all the holds to remake routes, send an employee up the wall with a spray bottle (containing soap and/or vinegar) and a cloth to wipe down the surface as they are lowered (Tension Climbing, 2024; Atomik Climbing Holds, 2026). Other gyms remove all the wall panels once a year and will powerwash the wall panels (E. Brulotte, personal communication, April 10, 2026). Both of these solutions result in infrequent cleanings or a lack of thorough sanitation.
Climbing holds themselves are cleaned when they are taken down from the wall and they are cleaned and sanitized either via powerwashing or a system involving a sort of dishwasher and UV sanitation (ClimbLab, 2022; KleanHolds, n.d.). 
While there exist no patents for cleaning climbing wall panels, there do exist patents for wall climbing cleaning mechanisms. During our patent search we found a rope mounted vertical cleaning machine (Won et al., 2022). We plan to use elements of this patent in our design, specifically making use of the rope mounting. There also exists a patent for a vertical climbing robot which utilizes a counter weighting system

We plan to make use of this counter weighting system or at least use it as a jumping off point. In addition to these two, there exists other vertically climbing cleaning robots. These are not compatible with climbing walls, however. Devices that use suction such as a wall climbing cleaning robot ((Xiefeng et al., 2022) would not be compatible with climbing walls and could cause damage. Devices that use liquid with no storage component for dirty water such as those used to clean windows on skyscrapers would not work since we must clean inside. Dirty water spraying would cause damage to mats below and other facilities. Our main development will be in creating a device that specifically works for this indoor, fragile environment. This existing technology will, however, serve as a good source of inspiration for our innovation and help define our niche.

Ease of use was our starting point for our specifications. Since current solutions are ineffective because of their time and labour intensive natures this specification is the main measure of how well our device will accomplish its goal. We are looking to take less than 15 minutes to set up the device and scrub down a panel, with the entire process being done by one person with as little effort-requirement as possible. This will make it viable for climbing gyms to clean panels each night after closing, either all at once or on a rotation. This goal is not achievable with current technology—our device will change this. 
Efficacy was the next point we went to since the main purpose of improving cleaning methods is to improve the cleanliness of the gym. We want to sanitize and clean. While chalk residue is mostly harmless, it is visible grime that not only looks bad, but can also affect the climbing experience. We aim to clean chalk and other grime off the walls. Sanitation is also an important part of our project. We aim to find at least an %80 reduction in bacteria growing on the walls of climbing gyms after cleaning with our device. We will test a wall on a random day at a local climbing gym by swabbing and growing a culture in the lab. We will also test the post standard cleaning procedure. We will compare these three to see how effective our device is. 
Damaging the wall or having the risk of damaging the wall with our device would discourage climbing gyms from buying our device. We want our product to provide only benefits to climbing gyms, a feat which would vastly increase the product’s marketability and use. Therefore, the possibility of the device damaging the wall through either incorrect use, device failure, or worse—standard device use—is an important metric for us to look at. We will examine the walls after using our device to determine if there is damage. 
Speed is a specification that greatly ties into ease of use. With ease of use we primarily target the device’s ability to solve the labor-intensive issue of current solutions. With speed we seek to resolve the time-intensive portion of the problem. While the two problems go hand-in-hand, we believe that adding this additional specification is important since there is a distinction between time and labour. We aim for our device to be able to clean down a section of the wall in 15 minutes or less. A typical wall section is 5-6 feet wide and up to around 60 feet tall, meaning the device should be able to clean 360 square feet of wall space within that 15 minutes of hands-on involvement from an employee.
Durability is very important for consumer use. While we aim to make our device cost effective, a device that constantly has to be replaced is one that will eventually no longer be used. We will aim for our device to be able to clean a gym 50+ times in its lifespan. This metric will realistically not be feasible for testing, though stress testing all functions of our device can provide the same result. We also want to make a device that does not have the risk of breaking due to improper use. Not all employees are always paying attention—our device should not have the risk of breaking down in regular, proper use.
Cost is a specification that will determine the marketability of our product. Our product needs to be economically sensible for climbing gyms to purchase. If it is an outrageous cost to add our device to the gym’s climbing routine, they will determine it not to be worth using. We aim to have our system cost no more than $300.
Safety, legality, and ethicality are metrics that must be adhered to. Our device should not be dangerous to the user during regular cleaning, our device must be legal to own and operate, and our device must be ethical. This remains true for our testing methods: we will obtain permission to use local climbing gyms to test our device and will do so safely and ethically.


Solutions and Alternatives
Solution 1: Cleaning Drapes

Figure 2. Mock-Up of Clean-O-Drapes
The cleaning drapes would be a set of curtains with microfiber towels that would be attached to a rail along the top of the climbing wall. The towels could be continuously soaked with a disinfectant, then pulled against the wall and moved back and forth all the way along the wall to clean the wall. This system would be effective at both cleaning visible residue and disinfecting, and would be a relatively passive system. There would likely be a fair amount of upfront investment from gyms in order to put a rail system above all of the walls, and storing the curtain system when not in use (and properly maintaining it by replacing the microfiber cloths) could be fairly difficult and expensive.

Solution 2: On Belay?

Figure 3. Mock-Up of On Belay?
On Belay? Would be a device with two oscillating brushes to clean off chalk residue, and a UV light to kill harmful bacteria. The device would be clipped into existing knots in climbing ropes that are attached to a hard anchor at the top of the wall. The device would be raised and then lowered down the wall as the brushes scrub and UV light disinfects the wall. This system would utilize existing infrastructure in the gym and be very easy for a single employee to operate, but it may take a little longer to clean each region of the gym, so the brushes would have to be wide enough to cover a whole route area.

Solution 3: Wallba

Figure 4. Mock-Up of Wallba
	The Wallba would be a suction powered device that adheres to the climbing wall and uses wheels to navigate the surface of the wall. It would have a vacuum component to remove chalk residue from the wall, as well as a component to spray disinfectant as it travels. This solution would be the most technically challenging to fabricate, as getting it to adhere to the wall would be an extremely difficult task from the get-go. Additionally, there is risk of the device damaging the textured surface of the climbing wall as it travels. The upsides of this solution would include that it could be run while the gym is closed, effectively resulting in no-hands on time for employees, and the potential to easily navigate the different contours of the wall if it is small enough.

Solution 4: Clean Stik

Figure 5. Mock-Up of Clean-Stik
	The Clean Stik would be a device that telescopes to reach even the tallest of walls. A scrubber and disinfectant sprayer could be attached to the end of the stick to allow employees to remove both chalk residue and harmful germs. This design is the most similar to the current state of the art, but it allows employees to stay on the ground as they clean the wall. The scrubber attachment could also be made larger to cover a greater portion of the wall, but that risks making the device unwieldy to use at the top portions of the wall. This would be the simplest of all the solutions.

We weighted ease of use, sanitation and cleaning ability, and damage-free by 3 to represent our most vital specifications. Ease of use and sanitation/cleaning ability are the main driving factors for the creation of our device. When comparing alternatives, a damage-free alternative also was very important to our group since it would both be easier to convince climbing gyms to allow us to test our product and to purchase our product if there was no risk to their facility. Climbing walls are typically a specifically textured surface with a thin plastic coating over a wooden backing, which can make proper cleaning difficult. 
Speed and durability were weighted by 2. Speed is very important to the project but due to its similarity with ease of use, we chose to weigh it below ease of use, though its importance still prompted us to weigh it. Durability was also a metric which our group believed to be important since an easily broken device can be costly both to the facility and to us during testing. Cost was not weighted since our team was fairly confident that a commercial climbing gym would have the budget to purchase any of the devices. This metric was still important for our decision making process, however. We came to our numbers by group vote, each of us provided scenarios and diagrams which demonstrated the ability of the device to adhere to certain specifications. Since we did not have time to test, this was our best method of determining the best device to make: the belay cleaning device. Our backup will be the cleaning drape. The main factor which brought us to the belayed device was our belief that it would be more effective and easier to use since it would actively scrub areas at a speed determined by the user. Furthermore, it would require less upfront investment by the climbing gym than the Clean-O-Drapes, which would require a rail system be installed.

Figure 6. Diagram and Drawing of the Proposed Best Solution

This device will be operated by the existing top-rope belay infrastructure and contain two or more oscillating brushes which are motor-powered. The device will move up and down via the rope system. When the wall goes inward, we theorize that a secondary rope could allow the device to move around the edge, or a passive weighting system could be used to push the device further into the wall. An attachment point for a hose to a Shop-Vac will be added into the system so that residue can be sucked away. Most climbing gyms already use Shop-Vacs or similar vacuums to clean climbing mats on the floor.
Other challenges to consider with this design is whether to use UV light or a disinfectant and scrub brush in order to sanitize the wall. If there is enough time, it may be worth exploring a segmented brush/scrubber design that could go over and around different contours on the wall, or even holds, so the solution could be used while holds are left on the wall.
